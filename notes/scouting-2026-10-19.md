The research behind lost-eclipses so far, from Foundation0's

- **Question.** Which eclipsing binaries catalogued from old photographic plates no longer eclipse in TESS?
- **In plain words.** In an eclipsing binary, two stars pass in front of each other and the light dips on a regular
  schedule. If a third star pulls on the pair, their orbit slowly tilts, and the eclipses can fade and stop. Many
  eclipsing binaries were found on photographic plates decades ago. TESS now watches most of the sky. A star that
  eclipsed then and doesn't now is a sign of a hidden third body.
- **Hub.** [astr0](https://github.com/Foundation-0/astr0).
- **Related.** [plate-eclipses](https://github.com/Foundation-0/plate-eclipses) (DASCH plates),
  [gaia-dr4](https://github.com/Foundation-0/gaia-dr4) (Gaia DR4 epoch photometry) and
  [eumemic/exoplanets](https://github.com/eumemic/exoplanets) (TESS light curves for 4 million stars).

## From the scouting report, not yet re-checked

- The General Catalogue of Variable Stars (GCVS, [VizieR B/gcvs](https://vizier.cds.unistra.fr/viz-bin/VizieR?-source=B/gcvs))
  has 13,319 eclipsing binaries. 3,280 of them have periods under 10 days and peak brighter than magnitude 12
  (queried 19 Oct 2026), so every TESS sector should show at least two eclipses.
- Borkovits et al. report vanished eclipses in two binaries in TESS's northern continuous viewing zone: "A Few Years
  Later: Revisiting Period Variations of Eclipsing Binaries in the Northern Continuous Viewing Zone of TESS"
  ([arXiv:2604.04004](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04004)). They also report them in Kepler triple systems: "Then and
  now: A new look at the eclipse timing variations of hierarchical triple star candidates in the primordial Kepler-field,
  revisited by TESS" ([arXiv:2502.09480](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.09480)).
- Their group (Konkoly) works within the Kepler and TESS continuous-viewing-zone samples. An arXiv search (eclipsing
  with disappeared, ceased or vanished: 91 hits) found no all-sky search against the old catalogues.
- Checks:
  - anyone can open the TESS light curve;
  - DASCH plates show the old eclipses, which also confirms the right star;
  - Gaia DR4 epoch photometry (2014–20) can date the change.
- Risks:
  - wrong identifications in the GCVS;
  - ellipsoidal or RS CVn variables misclassified as eclipsing;
  - blended TESS pixels.
- Kill criterion: none of the top 20 flags shows plate eclipses at the right star.
- Known stars whose eclipses stopped, such as SS Lac, make good controls.
